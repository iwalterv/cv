# Piloto #1: Why You Wouldn't Survive the Black Death

**Estado:** v2 (15-oct-2026), con los cambios de retención de `analisis-viral.md`. El usuario dio luz verde para producir ("dale"); antes de publicar revisará el video completo.
**Duración estimada:** ~10 min (1.650 palabras narradas a ~165 palabras/min). Con más de 8 minutos YouTube permite anuncios a mitad del video.
**Miniaturas (3 para Test & Compare de YouTube):** A) Red con un ramo de flores metido en la nariz, cara de pánico → **"FLOWERS?"**; B) Red mirando una moneda en el aire → **"50/50?"**; C) Red y una rata enorme mirándose → **"1348?"**. Palabra clave en rojo.
**Título alternativo (para pruebas A/B):** "You Wouldn't Survive the Black Death (Here's Why)".

Notas de producción:
- Lo que va en **negrita entre corchetes** es la indicación visual de la escena (no se narra).
- Pronunciación: *Melcombe* = "MEL-kum". *Yersinia pestis* = "yer-SIN-ee-uh PES-tiss". Si Holden lo lee mal, se escribe fonéticamente solo en el texto para la voz.
- **[MEDIDOR: XX %]** = el "Survival Meter": barra en una esquina con "YOUR ODDS" que sube o baja. Es un recurso de humor (cifras redondeadas a partir de los datos del guion) y lleva la nota pequeña "rough odds, for fun". Será la firma visual de la serie.
- Etiquetas de voz de ElevenLabs v4 entre corchetes en minúscula ([pause], [sarcastic], [whispers]): marcan el ritmo de los chistes. No se muestran en los subtítulos.
- Tono: irónico, cercano y nunca morboso. Nada de imágenes gráficas de cadáveres ni bubones: se sugiere, no se muestra (para los anunciantes).

## 0. Gancho (los primeros 30 segundos deciden todo)

**[Red en la cama de paja, sudando, mirándose la axila con horror]**
You wake up with a fever. There's a lump under your arm the size of an egg. [pause] Your neighbor is already dead.

**[Sabios de París con togas señalando el cielo con total seguridad]**
And the smartest doctors in Europe have just announced what's causing all this. [pause] Jupiter. [pause] And Saturn. And Mars. Basically, the sky.

**[Título en pantalla: ENGLAND, 1348. Aparece el medidor en la esquina]**
**[MEDIDOR: 100 %]**
Welcome to England, 1348. Today we're finding out exactly why you wouldn't survive the Black Death. And see that little meter in the corner? Those are your odds of making it to the end of this video. [sarcastic] Spoiler: they're going down.

**[Red mirando hacia un amanecer, con una moneda brillante]**
But stick around until the end, because the few people who *did* survive got something nobody in your village could have imagined.

## 1. Un año antes

**[Red, campesino, en una aldea inglesa en primavera; sonrisa nerviosa]**
But let's rewind a year. It's the spring of 1347. You're a peasant in a small village in the south of England, and honestly? Life is fine. [pause] Terrible, but fine.

**[Red con los bolsillos vacíos; castillo del señor al fondo]**
You own almost nothing. You owe your lord a couple of days of work in his fields every week, before you're allowed to touch your own. And dinner is bread. With a little pottage, if things are going well.

**[Mercaderes en un mercado medieval cuchicheando; Red escuchando]**
Then the merchants at the market start talking about a sickness in the East. By October 1347, it's in Sicily, arriving on ships at Messina. Within months, it's in Italy. Then France. Then everywhere ships can go.

**[Red encogiéndose de hombros en la taberna]**
And you do what everyone does. You assume it's somebody else's problem. England is an island. God is clearly on your side. And Sicily is very, very far away.

**[Red sonriendo; una sombra enorme detrás de él]**
[whispers] It is not that far away.

## 2. Llega

**[Dos barcos entrando en Melcombe, verano]**
In June 1348, according to a chronicle written by friars in King's Lynn, two ships land at Melcombe, in Dorset. On one of them is a sailor from Gascony, in France, and he's already sick.

**[Mapa de Inglaterra con Melcombe, Bristol y Southampton; los tres "levantando la mano"]**
The chronicle blames him. Historians aren't so sure it was that simple. Bristol and Southampton also have a claim to being first. Nobody wins this competition.

**[Línea roja que avanza por caminos y ríos de Inglaterra]**
From the coast, the plague moves the way everything moves in medieval England: along roads, rivers and markets. Bristol by the summer. London by the autumn. Over the next year, it reaches almost every corner of the country. Including your village.

**[MEDIDOR: 100 % → 50 %]**
**[Red mirando el medidor bajar de golpe]**
And just like that, about half the people around you are not going to make it. [pause] We haven't even gotten to the bad part.

**[Rata con pulga gigante (caricatura); Red retrocediendo]**
Now, you've probably heard that rats spread it. That's partly true. The disease is caused by a bacterium called *Yersinia pestis*, and it's carried by fleas. Fleas live on rats.

**[Familia de Red apretada en una choza de una sola habitación, con animales]**
But some researchers now think that in Europe, human fleas and body lice did a lot of the work, jumping from person to person in crowded, unwashed homes. And your home is very crowded. And very unwashed. You share one room with your whole family. Possibly also with the animals.

## 3. Los síntomas

**[Una pulga aterrizando en el brazo de Red, que no se da cuenta]**
So, let's say a flea finds you. Here's what happens next.

**[Red en la cama, con paño en la frente, tiritando]**
First, fever. A headache. Chills. Then a painful swelling appears in your groin, your armpit or your neck. It's called a bubo, and that's where the name "bubonic" comes from.

**[Red mirando un cartel de "probabilidades" con una moneda al aire]**
That's the bubonic version, and believe it or not, it's the "good" one. Even today, the World Health Organization estimates that untreated bubonic plague kills between 30 and 60 percent of the people who catch it. So it's basically a coin flip.

**[MEDIDOR: 50 %]**

**[Red tosiendo; otra persona dando un paso atrás]**
There's a worse version. If the infection reaches your lungs, it becomes pneumonic plague. Now it spreads through coughing, person to person, no flea required. Untreated, it's almost always fatal, sometimes within a day.

**[MEDIDOR: parpadea en rojo, 1 %]**

**[Red mirando a cámara]**
And in 1348, nothing is treated. Because nobody knows what's happening.

## 4. Los médicos

**[Red mirando un médico rico a lo lejos, tras una cerca]**
Let's talk about your doctors. Well, you're a peasant. You don't have a doctor. But the people who do aren't doing much better.

**[Sabios de la Universidad de París señalando planetas con un telescopio imaginario]**
In 1348, the King of France asked the top medical experts at the University of Paris to explain the plague. Their official answer? The planets. Specifically, a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars on the 20th of March, 1345, which supposedly corrupted the air.

**[Red tapándose la nariz en una nube verde de "aire malo"]**
That idea, poisoned air, or "miasma", was the best science of the time. So the cures actually make sense, in a terrible way.

**[Red con un ramo de flores pegado a la nariz]**
Carry flowers or herbs, so you're breathing something nicer.

**[Red rociando vinagre por la choza]**
Splash vinegar around. Burn something that smells strong.

**[Red mirando con miedo una tina de baño]**
Don't bathe too much. The Paris experts listed bathing right next to too much exercise and too much sex, as things that made your body more vulnerable. Honestly, for a medieval peasant, that one wasn't hard to follow.

**[Médico con un cuenco, haciendo una sangría; Red pálido]**
And of course, bloodletting, which was the medieval answer to almost everything. [sarcastic] Feeling weak and feverish? Let's remove some of your blood.

**[MEDIDOR: −10 %, con la nota "not helping"]**

**[El Papa en Avignon sentado entre fogatas, sudando]**
Even the Pope, Clement the Sixth, in Avignon, was advised by his physician to keep fires burning to purify the air. And to be fair, he survived. Maybe the fire helped. Maybe he was just lucky.

**[El clásico doctor de la peste con máscara de pico, tachado con una X roja]**
Oh, and that creepy bird mask you're imagining? The plague doctor with the long beak? Not here. That costume shows up in the 1600s, about three hundred years later.

**[Un médico medieval normal, nervioso]**
In 1348, the person treating you looks like... a guy. Probably a very nervous guy.

## 5. Todo se cae

**[Red mirando el medidor, que está muy bajo; reloj de arena a la mitad]**
We're about halfway through, and your odds are not looking great. But remember: if you make it to the end, things get weirdly good. First, though... it gets worse.

**[Red rezando en una iglesia de piedra]**
So the doctors can't help. What about the Church? For you, in 1348, the Church is everything. And the worst thing that can happen isn't dying. It's dying without confession.

**[Un sacerdote cansado yendo de casa en casa]**
Here's the problem. Priests visit the sick. Priests catch the plague. Priests die.

**[Obispo escribiendo una carta a la luz de una vela]**
So many that in January 1349, the Bishop of Bath and Wells sent an emergency message to his diocese: if no priest can be found, the dying can confess to an ordinary person. And if no man is available... even to a woman. For the medieval Church, that was a five-alarm fire.

**[Princesa con corona en un carruaje; nubes oscuras]**
Nobody is safe. Not even the top of society. In 1348, the King's own daughter, Princess Joan, died of plague near Bordeaux, on her way to marry a Spanish prince.

**[Dos mitras de arzobispo, una detrás de otra, cayendo]**
In 1349, the man chosen to be Archbishop of Canterbury died. Then his replacement died too, just weeks after taking the job.

**[Cementerio nuevo en Londres con zanjas largas, visto de lejos, sin cuerpos visibles]**
In London, the churchyards filled up. So the city opened new emergency cemeteries, with long trenches for the dead.

**[Científicos modernos con bata examinando un hueso; Red asomándose detrás]**
In 2011, scientists pulled the plague's DNA from skeletons buried at one of them, East Smithfield. More than six hundred and fifty years later, the culprit was finally confirmed.

## 5b. Tus opciones

**[Red frente a tres carteles: RUN / PRAY / BLAME]**
So, what can you actually do? Let's look at your options.

**[Nobles en carruaje huyendo hacia una villa en el campo]**
Option one: run. That's what the rich did. In Florence, the writer Giovanni Boccaccio described wealthy families leaving the city for their country houses and waiting it out. Great plan. If you have a country house.

**[Red con su saco, mirando un camino que no lleva a ninguna parte; el señor vigilando]**
You don't. You're tied to your lord's land. Leaving without permission can get you into serious trouble. And even if you did leave, where would you go? Every village is dealing with the same thing. And the plague is usually already there, waiting for you.

**[Procesión de flagelantes en Londres, vista de lejos; londinenses mirando con cara rara]**
Option two: pray harder. Across Europe, groups called flagellants marched from town to town, whipping themselves in public to beg God for mercy. In 1349, a group of them crossed over from Holland and Zeeland and put on a show in London. The Londoners watched. Mostly, they did not join in. Very English.

**[Pergamino con la palabra BLAME tachada; tono serio, sin violencia]**
Option three, and this is the dark one: blame someone. In many parts of Europe, terrified people blamed Jewish communities for poisoning the wells, and thousands of innocent people were murdered. In England, there were no Jewish communities left to blame. They had been expelled in 1290. It's one of the most shameful chapters of the whole pandemic, and a reminder that fear can be as deadly as any disease.

**[Red sentado, abrazando sus rodillas]**
So: you can't run, praying doesn't seem to work, and blaming people is horrifying. Which leaves you with option four. Wait. [pause] And hope.

**[Red señalando a la cámara; los tres carteles RUN / PRAY / WAIT]**
Be honest: which one would you have picked? Tell me in the comments.

## 6. Las cifras

**[Escribano medieval contando con los dedos, confundido]**
So, how bad was it really? Historians still argue about the exact numbers. Medieval record-keeping wasn't great, and it's hard to count when the people counting are also dying.

**[Gráfico simple: Inglaterra medio vacía]**
But most estimates for England land somewhere between 40 and 60 percent of the population, dead, in about two years. Across Europe, estimates range from about a third to more than half.

**[Ovejas y vacas sueltas por campos de trigo sin cosechar]**
And it's not just people. A chronicler in Leicester, Henry Knighton, wrote that sheep and cattle wandered loose through the fields and the crops, because there was no one left to drive them. Harvests rotted in the ground. There simply weren't enough hands.

**[La aldea de Red con 100 monigotes; la mitad se desvanece]**
Picture your village. A hundred people. The miller. The blacksmith. Your neighbors. The annoying guy who always borrows your plow without asking.

**[Red solo en la plaza de la aldea]**
By 1350, around half of them are gone. [pause] Statistically... so are you.

**[MEDIDOR: "≈ 50 %" se queda congelado]**

## 7. El giro: los que sobreviven

**[Red levantándose despacio, débil pero vivo, al amanecer]**
But let's say you're lucky. Let's say you're one of the people who make it. What's your life like now?

**[Red sorprendido, con un signo de interrogación]**
Weirdly... better.

**[MEDIDOR: se convierte en "IF YOU SURVIVED: WAGES ↑"]**

**[Campos vacíos; el señor buscando trabajadores; Red cruzado de brazos]**
Think about it. Half the workers are gone. The fields still need plowing. Suddenly, your lord needs you more than you need him. So peasants start asking for higher wages. And they get them.

**[Rey Eduardo III firmando un pergamino, enfadado]**
The people in charge hate this. In 1349, King Edward the Third issued the Ordinance of Labourers, and in 1351 Parliament passed the Statute of Labourers. Both tried to force wages back to what they were before the plague, and punished anyone who refused to work.

**[Red caminando con su saco hacia otra aldea con un cartel de "más paga"]**
It didn't really work. Wages kept creeping up. Workers moved to wherever paid better.

**[Red en la mesa con carne, cerveza y pan; sonrisa satisfecha]**
And over the following generations, many ordinary people ate more meat, drank more ale and lived better than their parents ever had.

**[Campesinos con horcas marchando, vistos de lejos]**
About thirty years later, in 1381, all that tension exploded into the Peasants' Revolt. But that's a story for another video.

**[Red mirando a cámara, levantando un trozo de carne]**
So congratulations, again. You probably wouldn't survive the Black Death. But if you did... you might finally get something more than bread.

## 8. Cierre

**[Niños jugando en ronda; un sello de "MYTH" en rojo]**
Oh, and one last thing. "Ring Around the Rosie"? Not about the plague. The rhyme first appeared in print in 1881, and folklorists only connected it to the plague in the twentieth century. Sorry.

**[Red con la bufanda roja, saludando; al lado, miniaturas del siguiente video]**
If you want to know what it was like to be the person who walked *into* plague houses on purpose, the next video is right here. And subscribe, because this little guy in the red scarf has a lot more centuries to suffer through.
