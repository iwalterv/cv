**Arranque nuevo (regla de la sección 6 de la investigación):**

- El medidor se explica en una sola frase.
- Una pregunta abierta (el hombre al que dejó su familia) y una nueva cada 60-90 s.
- Nada de "let's rewind".

**Miniaturas (Test & Compare), estilo v2: fondo plano, una sola situación, 1-3 palabras:**
- A) Red sentado en la cama con los ojos como platos, en plena noche; la familia duerme a su lado; luna en la ventana → **"2 SLEEPS?"**
- B) Red dormido boca arriba con un duendecillo peludo sentado sobre su pecho mirándolo → **"NIGHTMARE?"**
- C) Red en camisón, con una vela, asomado a la puerta del vecino, que también está despierto y con una vela → **"MIDNIGHT?"**

**No repetir el video #2:** allí ya salieron las velas de junco, el toque de queda, la cama de paja compartida y una frase sobre el primer y el segundo sueño. Aquí eso entra en una línea como mucho, y el foco es lo que se hacía despierto, los miedos, la ley nocturna y la prueba moderna.

Notas de producción: **[Escena entre corchetes]** = indicación visual, no se narra. **[MEDIDOR]** = "SUNRISE ODDS", tus probabilidades de ver el amanecer. Etiquetas de voz: [pause], [sarcastic], [whispers]. Paleta: azules nocturnos planos con la luz cálida de las velas, más simple que en los videos anteriores.

## 0. Gancho (0:00-0:30)

**[Habitación a oscuras, luz de luna; Red se incorpora de golpe en la cama con los ojos muy abiertos; reloj en pantalla: 12:00 AM]**
It's midnight. You went to bed four hours ago. And now you're wide awake.

**[Red mira a su alrededor: toda su familia también está sentada en la cama, despierta, tranquila]**
[pause] And so is everyone else in your house. Because for most of history, people didn't sleep through the night. [pause] They slept twice.

**[Plano general de un pueblo medieval de noche, cielo azul oscuro, sin una sola luz]**
**[MEDIDOR: 100 %]**
Welcome to night in medieval England. Up to sixteen hours of darkness, no light switch, and a whole list of things out there that want you. That meter in the corner? Your odds of seeing sunrise.

**[Silueta de un caballero dormido que se levanta blandiendo una espada; su familia huye por la puerta]**
And stick around, because one man's midnight habit got so bad, [pause] his own family moved out.

## 1. 6 PM: se pone el sol

**[Reloj: 6 PM. Red cerrando la puerta de su casa mientras el sol desaparece; el pueblo entero cerrando puertas]**
Let's start at sunset. In winter, that's before four in the afternoon. And for you, the day is basically over.

**[Red mirando una vela de junco minúscula, encogiéndose de hombros]**
Light costs money, and you don't have money. So you do what everyone does. You eat, you pray, and you go to bed. [pause] Early. Really early.

**[Cama de paja con toda la familia; Red apretado en medio]**
You already know the bed situation. Straw, wool blanket, the whole family. [sarcastic] Cozy.

**[Libro médico ilustrado: figura dormida sobre el lado derecho, una flecha curva hacia el izquierdo]**
And here's a detail you didn't expect. Medical books, from the late Middle Ages onward, told you exactly how to lie: on your right side for your first sleep, then turn to your left. It was supposed to help digestion.

**[Red girándose en la cama con cara de "¿en serio?"]**
Even your sleeping position had instructions.

## 2. 9 PM: el primer sueño y lo que hay en la oscuridad

**[Reloj: 9 PM. Red dormido; sobre su pecho, un duendecillo peludo sentado, mirándolo]**
**[MEDIDOR: 100 % → 90 %]**
So you fall asleep. And if you wake up feeling like something heavy is sitting on your chest, well, you have a word for that.

**[Manuscrito con una criatura pequeña sentada sobre un durmiente]**
The nightmare. And in the Middle Ages, it wasn't a bad dream. The "mare" was a creature, a spirit that sat on sleepers in the night. The word shows up in English around the year 1300, describing exactly that.

**[Monje escribiendo en un manuscrito a la luz de una vela, figuras fantasmales saliendo de la página]**
And the mare wasn't the only thing out there. Around 1400, a monk at Byland Abbey, in Yorkshire, filled the blank pages of a manuscript with local ghost stories.

**[Un fantasma pálido pidiendo algo a un campesino aterrado en un camino nocturno]**
And these weren't ghosts that went "boo". They were souls stuck in purgatory, and they wanted something. Usually, prayers. Sometimes, they wouldn't leave you alone until they got them.

**[Red en la puerta de su casa haciendo una bendición, rincón por rincón]**
So people protected their homes. In Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, written in the late 1300s, a carpenter says a "night-spell" at the four corners of his house and at the door. [pause] Basically, medieval home security.

**[MEDIDOR: 90 % → 80 %]**

## 3. Medianoche: te despiertas (a propósito)

**[Reloj: 12 AM. Toda la familia despierta en la cama, como en el gancho]**
And now, it's midnight. And you wake up. Not because of a noise. Not because of the ghost. [pause] Just because it's time.

**[Historiador moderno en un archivo, rodeado de pilas de documentos antiguos]**
Historian Roger Ekirch spent years digging through old diaries, court records and books, and found hundreds, and eventually thousands, of references to a "first sleep" and a "second sleep". In France, they called it "premier somme". In Italy, "primo sonno".

**[Página iluminada de Los cuentos de Canterbury; una princesa durmiendo y luego despierta, de noche]**
Even Chaucer mentions it. In one of his tales, a princess goes to bed just after dark, sleeps her "first sleep", and wakes up.

**[Red sentado en la cama en la oscuridad, pensativo]**
So between your two sleeps, you've got about an hour. Maybe more. In the dark. [pause] What do you do with it?

## 4. Lo que hacías en ese rato

**[Red arrodillado junto a la cama rezando, a la luz de la luna]**
Option one: you pray. This was considered a perfect time for it. Quiet, dark, nobody asking you for anything.

**[Monjes en fila con velas entrando en una iglesia de piedra en plena noche]**
Monks took it seriously. The Rule of Saint Benedict told them to get up at the eighth hour of the night in winter, around two in the morning, for the night prayers. Every night.

**[Red sentado, con cara de concentración, recordando un sueño: burbuja con un pez y una serpiente]**
Option two: you think about your dreams. Because to you, dreams meant something. The most popular dream book of the Middle Ages, the "Dream Book of Daniel", was basically an alphabetical list of things you might dream about, and whether they meant good luck or bad.

**[Red soplando las brasas del fuego del suelo; mirando a las vacas en la oscuridad]**
Option three: chores. Check the fire. Check the animals. Use the chamber pot, which lived under the bed. [pause] Yes, in the same room.

**[Padres medievales meciendo una cuna a medianoche, con cara de agotados]**
If you had a baby, this was feeding time. A French farce from the 1400s shows parents getting up at midnight to rock, walk and feed their child. Some things never change.

**[Pareja bajo la manta, solo se ven los pies; corazoncitos; Red mira a cámara incómodo]**
Option four. [pause] A French doctor in the 1500s, Laurent Joubert, wrote that working people had their best, ahem, "couple time" after the first sleep, because they weren't exhausted anymore. [sarcastic] Thank you, doctor. Very helpful.

**[Red con una vela llamando a la puerta del vecino, que abre también con una vela y en camisón]**
And option five: according to Ekirch's research, some people even got up and visited the neighbors. In the middle of the night. [pause] Imagine your neighbor knocking at one in the morning, and that's normal.

**[MEDIDOR: 80 % → 75 %]**

## 5. 1 AM: no salgas

**[Calle de un pueblo amurallado de noche; puertas de la ciudad cerrándose; guardias con lanzas]**
But here's the thing. You probably shouldn't go far. Because in medieval England, the night had its own rules.

**[Portón de una ciudad cerrado, con antorchas; seis guardias delante]**
The Statute of Winchester, in 1285, ordered the gates of walled towns closed from sunset to sunrise, and set a watch: in a city, six men at every gate, all night long.

**[Un vigilante con farol y lanza deteniendo a Red, que lleva una vela y cara de inocente]**
And if you were a stranger walking around at night? The watch could hold you until morning. If they still didn't like you then, they handed you over to the sheriff.

**[MEDIDOR: 75 % → 60 %]**

**[Red tropezando en un camino oscuro hacia una zanja, con su vela apagándose]**
And even without the law, the dark was dangerous on its own. No streetlights, unpaved roads, ditches, rivers. [pause] Getting lost on your way home was a real way to end your night. Permanently.

## 6. 3 AM: el hombre al que dejó su familia

**[Reloj: 3 AM. Castillo de noche; un caballero dormido se levanta de la cama con los ojos cerrados]**
Now, remember the man whose family moved out? [pause] Here he is.

**[El caballero, aún dormido, blandiendo una espada por la habitación; muebles volcados]**
His name was Pierre de Béarn, a French nobleman. The chronicler Jean Froissart wrote that after he killed a huge bear, Pierre started getting up in his sleep and grabbing his sword, fighting nobody.

**[El caballero buscando su espada en la oscuridad y gritando; los criados tapándose los oídos]**
And when he couldn't find it, he made such a racket that, in Froissart's words, it seemed like all the demons of hell were in there with him.

**[Su esposa y sus hijos saliendo de puntillas por la puerta con sus cosas]**
His family eventually left. [pause] Honestly? Fair.

**[MEDIDOR: 60 % → 55 %]**

## 7. El segundo sueño, y la prueba

**[Reloj: 3 AM. Red bostezando, metiéndose de nuevo bajo la manta]**
Anyway. After an hour or so, you get sleepy again, and you go back for your second sleep, until the sun comes up.

**[Laboratorio moderno de los años 90, de noche; voluntarios en camas, luces apagadas, un científico con portapapeles]**
And here's the crazy part. In the early 1990s, a psychiatrist named Thomas Wehr put volunteers in darkness for fourteen hours a day, for a month. No lamps, no screens.

**[Gráfico sencillo: una barra de sueño partida en dos bloques con un hueco en medio]**
By the fourth week, their sleep had split in two all by itself. About four hours of sleep, one or two hours awake, then four more hours. [pause] Just like the old records described.

**[Persona moderna despierta en la cama a las 3 AM mirando el móvil, preocupada]**
So if you sometimes wake up at three in the morning and can't sleep, maybe nothing's wrong with you. [pause] Maybe your body is just a little medieval.

**[Historiador con cara de duda, una lupa sobre un manuscrito]**
To be fair, not every historian agrees. Some think "first sleep" could just mean the first part of one sleep, and that the waking was short. But the experiment is hard to ignore.

## 8. Amanecer

**[Reloj: AMANECER. El sol sale sobre el pueblo; un gallo canta; Red sale por la puerta estirándose]**
**[MEDIDOR: 55 % → 70 %, "you made it"]**
And then, finally, the sun comes up. You survived the nightmare, the ghosts, the watch, the ditches and a neighbor knocking at one in the morning.

**[Red ante su campo con una azada, con ojeras]**
And now you get to work in the fields for the next ten hours. [pause] And tonight, you do it all again.

**[Red mirando a cámara]**
So, be honest. You wake up at midnight with an hour to kill. What are you doing? Praying, chores, or visiting the neighbors? Tell me in the comments.

**[Red con su bufanda roja, saludando a la luz del amanecer]**
And if you want to know what else medieval peasants did when nobody was watching, [pause] you know what to do.
