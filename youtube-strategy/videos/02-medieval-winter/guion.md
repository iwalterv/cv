# Video #2: Why You Wouldn't Survive a Medieval Winter

**Estado:** v1 (17-oct-2026), borrador para que el usuario lo apruebe **antes** de gastar en voz e imágenes.
**Por qué este tema:** análisis de `datos/viral-video2/README.md`. Es outlier en varios canales (Axen 65×, MTD 42× y 38×). La búsqueda "medieval winter survival" tiene una mediana de 262K, la versión animada de K12 sacó 1,76 M, y "how did medieval people survive winter" es la primera sugerencia del autocompletado. Es de temporada: publicar la última semana de octubre.
**Títulos para el A/B de YouTube:** A) "Why You Wouldn't Survive a Medieval Winter" (formato de la serie) · B) "How Did Medieval People Survive Winter?" (la búsqueda exacta).
**Duración estimada:** ~10 min.
**Estructura:** un calendario de **noviembre a marzo**. Cada mes es un capítulo, y el público se queda para ver si Red llega a la primavera.
**Miniaturas (Test & Compare), según `datos/miniaturas-analisis/`: personaje + amenaza + 2 o 3 palabras en amarillo:**
- A) Red tiritando, con escarcha en la bufanda, y detrás la puerta de la casa tapada de nieve con ojos de lobo en la oscuridad → **"-20°?"**
- B) Red en una cama de paja apretado entre cinco familiares, con una vaca asomando la cabeza por encima → **"THE COW?"**
- C) Red con un hacha mirando a un cerdo que lo mira a él, nieve cayendo → **"NOVEMBER?"**

Notas de producción (igual que el piloto):
- **[Escena entre corchetes]** = indicación visual, no se narra.
- **[MEDIDOR]** = Survival Meter. En este video el número es "tus probabilidades de llegar a la primavera".
- Etiquetas de voz [pause], [sarcastic], [whispers].
- **Variedad respecto del piloto:** no hay peste. El escenario es el invierno, mes a mes, con escenas nevadas, de interior, a la luz del fuego (otra paleta: azules y naranjas).
- Tono irónico, nunca morboso.

## 0. Gancho

**[Red, campesino, con la cara iluminada por un fuego en el centro de una choza llena de humo; tose]**
Your house has no chimney. Your bed is made of straw, and you're sharing it with your entire family. And just on the other side of the room, [pause] there's a cow.

**[Primer plano de la vaca masticando, mirando a cámara]**
That's not a punishment. That's your heating system.

**[Red mirando por la puerta: nieve sin fin]**
**[MEDIDOR: 100 %]**
Welcome to winter, in medieval England. You have five months to survive. And see that meter in the corner? Those are your odds of making it to spring. [sarcastic] They're about to have a very bad November.

**[Jarra de vino congelada sobre una balanza de mercado]**
And stick around, because in one of these winters, it got so cold that people were buying their wine [pause] by the pound.

## 1. Noviembre: el mes de la sangre

**[Calendario medieval ilustrado; la página de noviembre se tiñe de rojo]**
Let's start with November. The old English name for this month was Blōtmōnaþ. [pause] Blood month.

**[Red frente a su cerdo; el cerdo lo mira; Red esconde el cuchillo detrás de la espalda]**
And the name is honest. Around now, families slaughtered a lot of their animals. Not out of cruelty. Out of math.

**[Pajar casi vacío; vacas flacas mirándolo]**
Grass stops growing in winter. Hay is limited. You can't feed every animal until spring, so you keep the ones you need, the breeding stock and the ones that pull the plough, and the rest become dinner.

**[Red echando puñados de sal sobre trozos de carne en un barril]**
And because there's no fridge, the meat gets salted, smoked or dried. A family pig, killed around Saint Martin's Day, on the 11th of November, could be the difference between eating meat this winter and not.

**[Red mordiendo un trozo de carne salada; cara de que está durísima]**
[pause] Very, very salty meat. For months.

**[MEDIDOR: 100 % → 85 %]**

## 2. Tu casa

**[Corte transversal de la casa: una sola habitación, paredes de barro y palos, techo de paja, fuego en el centro del suelo]**
Now, let's look at your house. One main room. Walls of wattle and daub, which is woven sticks covered in mud and dung. A thatched roof. And in the middle of the floor, an open fire.

**[El humo subiendo y saliendo con dificultad por un agujero del techo; Red con los ojos llorosos]**
No chimney. Chimneys and fireplaces were spreading in richer homes, but in a lot of ordinary houses the smoke just drifted up into the roof and found its own way out. [pause] Eventually.

**[La familia sentada alrededor del fuego: la cara caliente, la espalda con escarcha]**
So you're always either too smoky or too cold. Usually both. Your face is roasting and your back is freezing.

**[Casa larga de Dartmoor: un pasillo en medio; a un lado la familia, al otro las vacas]**
And in some parts of England, like Dartmoor, people lived in longhouses. One long building, with the family at one end, a passage in the middle, and the cattle at the other end, under the same roof, especially in the coldest months.

**[Red durmiendo con una vaca resoplando cerca; un poco de vapor sale de la vaca]**
Is it smelly? Absolutely. But a cow is basically a big, warm radiator that eats grass. [pause] In January, you stop complaining about the smell.

**[Red poniéndose capa sobre capa hasta quedar redondo como una bola; solo asoma la bufanda roja]**
As for clothes, the strategy is simple: wear everything you own. A linen shirt, wool tunics on top of each other, wool hose on your legs, a cloak, and a hood if you have one. Women spun the wool themselves, so every layer you're wearing took someone in your family hours to make.

**[Red caminando por la nieve; sus zapatos de cuero empapados; deja huellas mojadas]**
And your shoes are thin leather. There are no rubber boots. Every time you go outside, your feet get wet. [pause] And there is no dryer. Just the smoky fire, and a lot of patience.

## 3. La oscuridad

**[Paisaje nevado; el sol se pone y en la esquina aparece "3:53 PM"]**
Here's something we forget. In the middle of an English winter, the sun sets before four in the afternoon. And then it's dark. Really dark.

**[Red sujetando un junco encendido; luz débil y temblorosa]**
No electricity, obviously. Good candles were made of beeswax, and those were for churches and rich people. Beeswax could cost about three times as much as tallow, which is candles made from animal fat.

**[Red mojando juncos pelados en grasa caliente]**
And a lot of poor families couldn't even afford that. They used rushlights: dried rushes dipped in fat, burning for a few minutes each. [pause] Your whole evening, lit by a greasy plant.

**[Campanario de un pueblo sonando de noche; Red tapando el fuego con una tapa de barro]**
And then a bell rings, and it's time to cover the fire, to keep it from burning down the house, and the whole village with it. That bell had a name in Old French: *cuevrefeu*. [pause] "Cover fire". Which is where we get the word "curfew".

**[MEDIDOR: 85 % → 70 %]**

## 4. La cama

**[La familia entera apretada en un jergón de paja en el suelo; Red en el medio, aplastado]**
So, bedtime. Your mattress is a sack of straw, on the floor or up in a loft. And you're not alone in it. Families slept together, huddled up, because body heat is free.

**[Red en la oscuridad con los ojos muy abiertos; todos los demás roncando]**
And here's where it gets interesting. According to historian Roger Ekirch, for centuries, Europeans often didn't sleep in one long block. They had a "first sleep", then woke up in the middle of the night for an hour or so, and then went back for a "second sleep".

**[Gente despierta a medianoche: uno reza, otra remienda ropa, dos vecinos charlan en voz baja]**
In that gap, people prayed, did small chores, talked, visited neighbors, and, well, [pause] made more peasants.

**[Red mirando a cámara en la oscuridad]**
With fourteen hours of darkness, you'd probably wake up in the middle too.

## 5. Diciembre: por fin, una fiesta

**[Pueblo nevado de noche; la casa del señor iluminada y con humo por la chimenea]**
Then comes December, and the best news of the whole winter: Christmas. And for you, Christmas is a big deal, because it's one of the few times in the year when the work actually stops.

**[Red caminando hacia la casa del señor con dos gallinas bajo el brazo]**
On a lot of manors, tenants owed the lord some rent at Christmas, often paid in food. Hens, bread, ale. [pause] So you walk up to the big house, holding your chickens.

**[Red llegando a la fiesta con su propio plato y un tronco bajo el brazo]**
And on some manors, there was a feast for the tenants. But the tradition, according to some accounts, was that you brought your own plate. And your own firewood.

**[Red sentado en la mesa larga, comiendo carne, con una sonrisa gigante]**
Still, it's meat, it's warm, and nobody is making you work. Twelve days of Christmas, right through to Twelfth Night.

**[Calendario: lunes después de Reyes; Red con el arado, cara de lunes]**
And then, on the first Monday after, the party's over. In parts of England this was called Plough Monday. [pause] Which tells you exactly what you're doing that day.

**[MEDIDOR: 70 % → 75 %, primera subida]**

## 6. Enero y febrero: las grandes heladas

**[El Támesis congelado; gente caminando y a caballo sobre el hielo]**
Now we get to the hard part. Some medieval winters were brutal. In 1410, according to a London chronicle, a frost lasted fourteen weeks and the Thames froze so solid that people could walk, and even ride horses, across it.

**[Puente de Londres con arcos rotos por bloques de hielo]**
In the winter of 1281 to 1282, when the ice finally broke, it smashed arches of London Bridge. People had been crossing the river on foot. [pause] Dry-shod.

**[Mercado: el vendedor pesa un bloque de vino congelado en una balanza]**
And the winter of 1205 is the one I promised you. A frost that started in mid-January and lasted until late March. No one could work the ground. And, according to an old account of great English frosts, ale and wine froze so hard that they were sold [pause] by weight.

**[Red golpeando un bloque de cerveza helada con un martillo]**
You haven't truly lived until you've had to chew your beer.

**[Barcazas atrapadas en el hielo; carros llevando mercancías por tierra hacia Londres]**
In the winter of 1434, the Thames froze from London Bridge all the way down to Gravesend, from Christmas Day to the 10th of February. Ships couldn't get through, so goods had to be hauled into London overland. [pause] Which, in medieval roads covered in snow, is about as fun as it sounds.

**[Miniatura de febrero de Les Très Riches Heures: campesinos calentándose junto al fuego, nieve, un redil con ovejas]**
And if you want to see what February looked like back then, there's a famous painting. In a French prayer book made for a duke around 1412 to 1416, *Les Très Riches Heures*, the page for February shows a snowy farm. Sheep huddled in their pen. A man hauling firewood. And peasants inside, sitting as close to the fire as humanly possible, trying to thaw out. [pause] Six hundred years later, it's still the most relatable painting of winter ever made.

**[Red mirando sus semillas en un saco: si no se puede arar, no hay cosecha]**
And here's the real danger. When the ground freezes for months, you can't plough or sow. And a late spring means a late, small harvest. Which means next winter, [pause] you start with even less.

**[MEDIDOR: 75 % → 40 %]**

## 7. Marzo: el hueco del hambre

**[El granero casi vacío; un solo saco; los barriles de carne salada vacíos]**
If you make it to March, congratulations. You've reached the most dangerous part. Historians sometimes call it the hungry gap.

**[Red raspando el fondo de un barril; una sola cebolla]**
Your salted meat is running out. The grain is running low. And the new crops won't be ready for months. The first wild greens are only just starting to show up.

**[Red ante una iglesia con un cartel de cuaresma; plato con un arenque salado]**
And the Church timing doesn't help: this is also Lent, forty days of fasting with no meat. So your diet becomes [pause] salted herring. And hope.

**[MEDIDOR: 40 % → 25 %]**

## 8. El peor caso

**[Cielo gris, lluvia sin fin sobre campos inundados]**
And sometimes, it's not one bad winter. It's a string of bad years. Starting in 1315, it rained, and rained, and stayed cold, across Northern Europe. Crops rotted in the fields, year after year.

**[Mapa del norte de Europa, de Irlanda a Polonia, oscureciéndose]**
It became known as the Great Famine. It hit a region of around thirty million people, from Ireland to Poland, and the crops kept failing until the harvest of 1317.

**[Campo con unas pocas vacas; muchas sombras de vacas que desaparecen]**
And right after it came a cattle plague that, in some places, killed most of the herds. [pause] The cows that kept you warm, and alive.

**[Red mirando un cielo cada vez más frío; un termómetro imaginario bajando]**
Some historians see this as the start of a colder era in Europe, often called the Little Ice Age. Exactly when it began is still debated, but your grandchildren's winters [pause] weren't getting any easier.

**[MEDIDOR: parpadea en rojo, 5 %]**

## 9. La primavera

**[La nieve se derrite; un brote verde; Red sale de la casa entrecerrando los ojos al sol]**
But let's say you made it. The snow melts. The days get longer. The first green shoots come up. The cow, somehow, is still alive.

**[MEDIDOR: 5 % → 60 %, nota "you made it"]**

**[Red respirando aire fresco, sin humo, feliz]**
You've survived five months of smoke, salt, darkness, frozen beer and a bed shared with six people and a cow. [pause] And in about seven months, you get to do it all again.

**[Red mirando a cámara]**
So, be honest: how many months would you have lasted? November? February? Tell me in the comments.

**[Red con su bufanda roja, saludando desde la nieve]**
And if you want to find out what else you wouldn't survive, you know what to do. [pause] Red needs all the help he can get.
