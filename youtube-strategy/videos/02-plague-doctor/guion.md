## 0. Gancho

**[Red con toga de médico firmando un pergamino enorme; detrás, una ciudad italiana con humo]**
Congratulations. You just got the best-paying job in town. [pause] Four times what a normal doctor makes.

**[Primer plano del contrato; las cláusulas se iluminan una a una]**
There are just a few conditions. You can only treat one kind of patient. You can't leave. And when you're done with them, you get locked away from everyone else.

**[18 sillas de médicos en fila; se van vaciando una por una]**
Oh, and in Venice, out of eighteen doctors, one was still working. [pause] The rest? Dead, or gone.

**[Aparece el medidor en la esquina]**
**[MEDIDOR: 100 %]**
Welcome to the worst job in history. Today, you're a plague doctor. And yes, the meter's back. Those are your odds of making it to the end of this video. [sarcastic] Good luck, doctor.

**[Silueta del médico de pico en la niebla]**
And by the end, you'll find out that the mask everyone pictures when they think of you? [pause] You never wore it.

## 1. La oferta de trabajo

**[Red en una plaza de Orvieto, 1348; vecinos tapándose la nariz]**
Let's start in 1348, in Orvieto, a hill town in central Italy. The Black Death has just arrived, and the town has a problem: the people who are supposed to fix it keep running away.

**[Médicos ricos subiendo a carros con sus maletas, saliendo de la ciudad]**
Doctors back then were usually paid by whoever could afford them. And when the plague came, a lot of them did the reasonable thing. They packed up and left.

**[Red ante el consejo de la ciudad; bolsa de monedas sobre la mesa]**
So the town does something new. It hires its own doctor. A man named Matteo Angelo gets a contract for four times the normal salary. A regular doctor made about fifty florins a year. [pause] Matteo was offered two hundred.

**[Red mirando la bolsa, luego la ciudad en llamas, luego otra vez la bolsa]**
That's how you know a job is bad. Nobody pays four times the going rate for something nice.

## 2. El contrato

**[El contrato desenrollándose como un pergamino infinito]**
Over the next three hundred years, towns all over Europe copied the idea. And the contracts got very specific.

**[Red con una pluma; tachando con cara de miedo cada punto]**
You treat plague patients. Only plague patients. No rich merchant with a sore knee, no easy money on the side.

**[Red en un cuarto vacío con un candado en la puerta]**
After you see the sick, you might have to stay in quarantine, away from everyone else. [pause] Which, when you think about it, is the town admitting it expects you to catch it.

**[Red escribiendo nombres en un libro enorme, la lista no termina]**
And a big part of the job isn't even medicine. It's paperwork. You count the dead and write down their names for the town records.

**[Red junto a una cama, escribiendo mientras alguien dicta]**
You also write down people's last wishes, because there's no lawyer brave enough to come in. And in some cities, like Florence and Perugia, they ask you to open up the bodies to figure out what's going on.

**[MEDIDOR: 100 % → 70 %]**

## 3. Tu formación

**[Red en un aula medieval; profesor señalando un diagrama de los cuatro humores]**
Now, you are a trained doctor. You went to university. You studied the great authorities: Galen, Hippocrates, the four humors. [pause] Which is a problem.

**[Cuatro frascos: sangre, flema, bilis amarilla, bilis negra; uno burbujeando]**
Because according to everything you've learned, disease comes from your body's four fluids getting out of balance. And the plague, specifically, comes from bad air.

**[Nube verde de "mal aire" saliendo de un pantano y de un montón de basura]**
It's called miasma. Rotting things poison the air, you breathe it in, and it corrupts your insides. So, to protect yourself, you're going to need to smell nice. [pause] Really, really nice.

**[Red frente al cielo nocturno; Saturno, Júpiter y Marte alineados]**
And if you're wondering where the bad air came from, one of the most famous surgeons of the time had an answer. The planets. Saturn, Jupiter and Mars lined up on the 24th of March, 1345. [sarcastic] Obviously.

## 4. El hombre que se quedó

**[Palacio de los Papas de Aviñón; Red con toga de médico papal]**
That surgeon was Guy de Chauliac, doctor to Pope Clement the Sixth in Avignon. And he left us something rare: a first-hand account of what it was like.

**[Calles vacías de Aviñón; puertas cerradas]**
He wrote that the plague hit Avignon in January 1348 and lasted seven months. That it was so contagious, people died without servants and were buried without priests. That a father would not visit his son, and a son would not visit his father.

**[Guardias en la puerta de una ciudad obligando a un viajero a tragarse un frasco de polvos]**
People were so terrified that guards were posted at the city gates, letting in only people they knew. And if they caught a stranger carrying powders or ointments, they assumed it was poison [pause] and made him swallow it.

**[Red, médico, escondiendo rápidamente su maletín detrás de la espalda]**
Which is bad news for you, because your entire job is carrying powders and ointments.

**[Médicos asomándose por una ventana, sin atreverse a entrar]**
And he was brutally honest about his own profession. Doctors, he wrote, didn't dare visit the sick. And when they did, they could do almost nothing. [pause] And they were paid nothing.

**[Red con cara de "¿entonces por qué me quedo?"]**
So why did he stay? In his own words: to avoid a bad reputation. [pause] That's it. Not courage. Not science. He just didn't want people talking.

**[Red en la cama, con fiebre; amigos alrededor con cara de despedida]**
And near the end of the outbreak, he caught it. A fever, a swelling in the groin, six weeks in bed. His friends were sure he was going to die.

**[MEDIDOR: 70 % → 20 %]**

**[Red sentándose en la cama, vivo, sorprendido]**
[pause] He didn't. He survived, went back to work, and wrote it all down. So there's hope. A little.

## 5. Tu maletín

**[Red abriendo un maletín de médico; dentro, una cuchilla, frascos y un tarro]**
So, doctor. Let's open your bag and see what you've got.

**[Red leyendo un libro de medicina; la página dice en grande "FLEE"]**
First, prevention. And here, the best surgeon in Europe has a clear recommendation. According to Guy de Chauliac, there was nothing better than to leave the area before the plague arrived. [pause] The number one medical advice was: run. Which is great advice for everyone except, you know, the doctor.

**[Red sosteniendo un cuenco mientras un paciente sangra del brazo; nada gráfico]**
Option one: bloodletting. The idea is to drain off the bad humors. [pause] The actual effect is a weaker patient.

**[Un tarro lujoso que dice THERIAC, con una serpiente en la etiqueta]**
Option two: theriac. The miracle drug of the Middle Ages. A paste of around sixty ingredients, mixed with honey. Including opium. And powdered viper. [pause] In Venice, it was considered so important that it had to be made in public, so everyone could see nobody was cheating on the recipe.

**[Red sosteniendo un cataplasma humeante; ingredientes flotando alrededor]**
And option three, straight from Guy de Chauliac himself. To treat the swellings, you take figs and onions, cook them, mash them, and mix them with bread dough and butter. Then you put it on the lump.

**[Red mirando la cataplasma, luego a la cámara]**
[pause] You are, technically, making a sandwich. [sarcastic] Medicine.

**[MEDIDOR: 20 % → −5 %, con la nota "not helping"]**

**[Nostradamus con barba y sombrero, rodeado de rosas, en una ciudad de Provenza]**
To be fair, not every plague doctor was this bad. In 1546, the city of Aix, in the south of France, called in a doctor to fight an outbreak. His name? [pause] Nostradamus. Yes, that Nostradamus.

**[Nostradamus negándose a una sangría con la mano levantada]**
Before he was famous for predictions, he was a plague doctor. And he did something unusual: he was against most of the violent treatments of his day, including bloodletting. Instead, he handed out pills made mostly of rose petals.

**[Red mirando una píldora de rosa]**
Did the roses cure anyone? Almost certainly not. But by not draining his patients' blood, he probably did them less harm than everyone else. [pause] In plague medicine, that counts as a win.

## 6. El traje (que no usaste)

**[Silueta clásica del médico de pico; Red la mira y niega con la cabeza]**
Now let's talk about the outfit. The bird mask. The long black coat. The hat. You're picturing it right now. [pause] And in 1348, you would not have been wearing it.

**[Línea de tiempo: 1348 al principio, el traje aparece muy a la derecha, en el siglo XVII]**
That costume shows up about three hundred years later. The story usually goes back to Charles de Lorme, a doctor to King Louis the Thirteenth of France, who supposedly designed it during an outbreak in 1619.

**[Red con lupa sobre un libro; signo de interrogación]**
But historians are careful with that, because it comes from an account written later, and it's pretty flattering to de Lorme. The first pictures we actually have are from 1656, during a plague in Rome.

**[Desglose del traje con etiquetas: abrigo encerado, guantes, sombrero, ojos de vidrio, bastón]**
Here's what it was supposed to be: a coat covered in wax, leather gloves and boots, a hat, glass eyes, and a stick, so you could examine patients without touching them.

**[El pico abierto como un cajón: rosas, lavanda, menta, alcanfor, una esponja con vinagre]**
And the beak, about half a foot long, was stuffed with sweet-smelling stuff: dried roses, lavender, mint, camphor, a sponge soaked in vinegar, or theriac. [pause] Remember the bad air? The beak was basically a perfume filter.

**[Grabado satírico de "Doctor Schnabel" pegado en una pared, gente riéndose]**
Even back then, people found it a little ridiculous. One of the most famous images, the German print "Doctor Beak of Rome", was a satire. And only two of these masks are known to survive today, both in Germany.

**[Red con el traje; una pulga rebota en el cuero]**
Here's the twist, though. Some historians think it might have helped. Not because of the perfume. Because all that leather and wax made it harder for fleas to reach your skin. [pause] Right result. Completely wrong reason.

**[MEDIDOR: −5 % → 30 %]**

## 7. Edimburgo, 1645

**[Edimburgo, calles empinadas y oscuras; casas con una bandera blanca en la ventana]**
And for the people who did wear it, the job didn't get much better. Let's jump to Edinburgh, in 1645.

**[Consejo municipal contratando a un médico; firma]**
In December 1644, as plague closed in on the city, the council hired a doctor called John Paulitious. Forty pounds Scots a month.

**[La bolsa de dinero crece; la cara de Red cada vez más pálida]**
As the outbreak got worse, they kept raising his pay to keep him working. Double, and then more.

**[Silla vacía con el sombrero del médico encima]**
[pause] It didn't matter. Within a few months, John Paulitious was dead. Of the plague.

**[MEDIDOR: parpadea en rojo, 1 %]**

**[Red con el traje de pico completo, firmando el siguiente contrato]**
His replacement, George Rae, wore the full leather outfit. He survived. [pause] And then he had to fight the council over the money they'd promised him. Accounts disagree on whether he ever got all of it.

**[Red mirando la bolsa vacía]**
So, to recap: you survive the plague, and then you have to survive city hall.

## 8. Por qué tu cara está en todas partes

**[Escenario de teatro italiano con el personaje del médico de pico; público riendo]**
Here's the strange part. The plague doctor lost almost every battle. And still, he's the one we remember.

**[Carnaval de Venecia, máscara blanca de pico entre la multitud]**
He became a character in Italian comedy. A mask at the Carnival of Venice. A Halloween costume. A video game villain.

**[Red en el traje, mirando sus propias copias en una tienda de disfraces]**
Not because the medicine worked. It didn't. [pause] But because, when everyone else ran, this was the person who walked toward the sick. For money, sure. To avoid a bad reputation, maybe. But they walked in.

**[MEDIDOR: se queda en "≈ 30 %"]**

## 9. Cierre

**[Red se quita la máscara; debajo, su cara cansada de siempre]**
So, would you take the job? Four times the pay. Plague patients only. No leaving town. Figs and onions in your bag. [pause] Tell me in the comments.

**[Red con bufanda roja mirando hacia una ventana con nieve]**
And next time, we're leaving the plague behind and going somewhere colder. Much colder. Because surviving a medieval winter might be even harder than surviving your doctor.
